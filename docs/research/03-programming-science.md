## 0. TL;DR rules for the coaching skill

| Variable | Default prescription | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly hard sets per muscle (hypertrophy) | **10–20** fractional sets/muscle/week for most people. 4–6 is a solid minimum. Above 20 can add a little for advanced lifters if they recover from it. | Schoenfeld 2017; Baz-Valle 2022; Pelland 2025 |
| Set counting | Direct set = 1. Set where the muscle is a synergist (e.g. triceps in bench) = 0.5 ("fractional"). | Pelland 2025 [V] |
| Weekly sets per lift (strength) | Diminishing returns come sooner than for hypertrophy. About 3–6 heavy sets/lift/week is a minimum effective dose. More helps, but the curve flattens fast. | Androulakis-Korakakis 2021; Pelland 2025 |
| Frequency | Train each muscle **≥2x/week** when the total volume is moderate–high (it spreads volume, about 6–10 sets per session per muscle is plenty). Once volume is equated, frequency barely matters for hypertrophy. Practising lifts more often helps strength. | Schoenfeld 2019; Grgic 2018; Pelland 2025 |
| Load / reps | Hypertrophy works with any load from about **30–85% 1RM (≈5–30 reps)** when sets go near failure. Practical default is **6–15 reps** for compounds and 10–20 for isolations. Strength needs heavy loads (**≥80% 1RM, 1–6 reps**). | Schoenfeld 2017; Lopez 2021; Currier 2023 |
| Proximity to failure | Hypertrophy: **0–3 RIR** (most sets 1–3 RIR, last set of isolations can go to 0). Strength: **2–5 RIR** is fine. Failure is not required. | Refalo 2023; Robinson 2024 |
| Rest | **≥90 s–3 min** for compounds and 60–120 s for isolations. Below 60 s costs some hypertrophy. Beyond about 90 s there is no clear extra hypertrophy, but longer rest helps strength or volume on heavy compounds. | Singer 2024 [V] |
| ROM | Full ROM that emphasises the **stretched (long-length) position**. Lengthened partials ≥ shortened partials, and roughly equal to full ROM. | Wolf 2023; Pedrosa 2022; Kassiano 2023; Maeo 2022 |
| Progression | Novice: linear (add load each session or week). Intermediate: double progression plus RIR-based autoregulation. Track e1RM. | Helms/Zourdos RIR-RPE |
| Deload | Optional, not mandatory. Use one every **4–8 weeks** (survey mean 5.6 wk) or when performance drops. Cut volume about 30–50%, keep frequency, lower load or RIR modestly, keep exercises. **Do not stop training completely** (it may cost strength). | Rogerson/Bell 2024 [V]; Coleman 2024 [V]; Bell 2023 Delphi [V] |
| Protein | **1.6 g/kg/day** captures most of the benefit. **Up to 2.2 g/kg** covers the upper CI. 2.3–3.1 g/kg lean mass in a hard cut. 0.25–0.4 g/kg per meal across 3–5 meals. | Morton 2018 [V]; ISSN 2017 [V] |
| Creatine | Creatine monohydrate, **3–5 g/day** (optional load of 0.3 g/kg/day for 5–7 days). Small, real gains in lean mass and strength. Safe. | ISSN 2017 (Kreider); Burke 2023 [V] |
| Cardio | Does **not** meaningfully blunt hypertrophy or max strength (Schumann 2022). Risks are a small cost to explosive power, plus running at high frequency or duration done in the same session. Separate from lifting by ≥3–6 h or do it on separate days if possible. Cycling interferes less than running. | Schumann 2022 [V]; Wilson 2012 [V]; Petré 2021 |
| Sleep | ≥7 h per night. Sleep restriction lowers muscle protein synthesis. | Saner 2020 [V] |

## 1. Hypertrophy

### 1.1 Weekly volume (sets per muscle per week): dose-response

**Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos — "The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains." Sports Medicine (online Dec 2025; preprint SportRxiv Oct 2024). DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w** [V for abstract, S for coefficients]
- 67 studies, about 2,058 participants. Multilevel Bayesian meta-regressions.
- **Volume quantification:** three methods were compared ("direct only", "total", and "fractional", where indirect or synergist sets count as 0.5). **Fractional counting had the strongest relative evidence** and was used for the primary models. Coaching rule: count compound sets as 1 set for prime movers and 0.5 for synergists.
- **Hypertrophy and volume:** posterior probability of a positive slope = 100%. The best-fit model showed **diminishing returns**, but they were *gradual* (secondary summaries report a square-root-type curve). Stronger by Science cites a linear marginal effect of about **+0.24% muscle size per additional weekly set**, larger at low volumes and smaller at high volumes. **No clear plateau was found within the studied range** (studies reached the 30–40+ set range, but there are few data points there).
- **Strength and volume:** also positive (100% probability), but diminishing returns are **much more pronounced**. Most of the strength benefit comes from the first few sets per week (secondary readings say the curve flattens around 4–5 fractional sets per week). Treat that specific number as approximate.
- **Frequency:** the effect on hypertrophy, with volume adjusted, was **compatible with negligible** (posterior probability < 100%). The effect on strength was **clearly positive** (it held after adjusting for volume and training status). Frequency is therefore a strength or skill lever, and for hypertrophy it is a *volume-distribution tool*.
- Secondary analysis (MASS / Pelland podcast): per-session volume above about 6–11 fractional sets per muscle shows diminishing returns. Spread higher weekly volumes across ≥2 sessions.
- Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/ ; https://www.strongerbyscience.com/volume/ ; https://fitchef.com/studies/training-volume-sets-per-week-study/ (secondary)

**Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci)**: 15 studies / 34 treatment groups. Graded dose-response: **<5, 5–9, and 10+ sets/week**. 10+ sets gave the most growth, at about **+0.37% per added weekly set**. This was the basis for the "10–20 sets" guideline. [S]

**Baz-Valle, Balsalobre-Fernández, Alix-Fages, Santos-Concejero 2022 (J Hum Kinet)**: moderate (12–20) vs high (>20) weekly sets in trained subjects. **No significant overall difference** for quads or biceps; an advantage for >20 sets appeared only for the triceps. Interpretation: 12–20 captures most of the gain, and >20 is for individuals who respond to and tolerate it. [S]

**Israetel (Renaissance Periodization) volume landmarks [P]**
- MV (maintenance volume): about 4–6 sets/muscle/week. Volume can drop as low as about 1/3 of normal for maintenance if intensity is held.
- MEV (minimum effective volume): about 6–10 sets/week for most muscles (lower for muscles that get a lot of indirect work, such as front delts and triceps).
- MAV (maximum adaptive volume): about 12–20 sets/week.
- MRV (maximum recoverable volume): about 20–26+ sets/week. This varies widely between individuals and muscles.
- Mesocycle model: start a block near MEV, add about 1–2 sets per muscle per week toward MRV over 3–6 weeks, deload, then repeat. This lines up with the RCT and meta-regression picture (positive, diminishing dose-response). The exact per-muscle numbers are heuristics, not measured values.
- Source (secondary listings): https://arvo.guru/resources/volume-landmarks ; original: renaissanceperiodization.com "Training Volume Landmarks for Muscle Growth".

**Practical coaching rule:** beginners use 6–10 sets/muscle/week. Intermediates use 10–20. Advanced lifters prioritise lagging muscles at 15–25 and keep others at maintenance (about 6–10). Increase volume only if performance is progressing and recovery holds. If progress has stalled and recovery is fine, add 2–4 sets/week to the muscle.

### 1.2 Frequency
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Krieger 2019 (J Sports Sci), 25 studies:** when volume is equated, frequency does **not** significantly affect hypertrophy. When volume is not equated, higher frequency helps, because it adds volume. [S]
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger 2016 (Sports Med):** ≥2x/week beat 1x/week (with mostly volume-confounded data). This is where "2x/week minimum" comes from. [S]
- **Grgic, Schoenfeld, Latella 2019:** once-weekly can match 2–3x when volume is equated (direct site-specific measures). [S]
- **Pelland 2025:** frequency has a negligible-to-small effect on hypertrophy and a clear positive effect on strength. [V]
- **Rule:** 2–3x/week per muscle by default (best for distributing 10–20 sets and practising lifts). 1x/week is acceptable for maintenance or for low volumes.
- Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/ ; https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/

### 1.3 Load / rep range
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn, Krieger 2017 (J Strength Cond Res):** 21 studies. Low load (≤60% 1RM) vs high load (>60%), with sets to failure. **Hypertrophy is similar. 1RM strength is better with high load.** Studies used loads down to about 30% 1RM. [S/V]
- **Lopez et al. 2021 (Med Sci Sports Exerc), network meta-analysis:** 28 studies, n=747. Low (>15RM), moderate (9–15RM) and high (≤8RM) loads to failure. **No hypertrophy difference between loads.** Strength is better with high vs low load (SMD 0.60–0.63), and high vs moderate shows a trend (SMD 0.26–0.28, p=0.068). [V] https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929
- **Currier et al. 2023 (BJSM), Bayesian network MA:** 178 studies for strength (n=5,097) and 119 for hypertrophy. **All RT prescriptions beat control. Hypertrophy was similar across prescriptions.** The top-ranked hypertrophy option was higher load, multiple sets, 2x/week. **Strength was best with higher loads.** The top-ranked strength option was higher load, multiple sets, 3x/week. [V] https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/18/1211 ; DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807
- **Practical caveats:** very light loads (>25–30 reps) are uncomfortable, hard to judge RIR on, and limited by cardio or discomfort. A *practical* default for hypertrophy is **6–12 reps for heavy compounds and 8–20 for isolations or machines**. Lower-rep sets cost more fatigue per unit of stimulus on axial compounds. Higher-rep sets are more prone to RIR misjudgement.

### 1.4 Proximity to failure / RIR
- **Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med), "Influence of RT proximity-to-failure on skeletal muscle hypertrophy":** 15 studies. **No clear hypertrophy advantage for training to momentary failure** vs non-failure when volume is accounted for. The relationship may be non-linear. [V] https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796
- **Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, ... Zourdos 2024 (Sports Med 54:2209–2231), "Exploring the dose-response relationship between estimated RIR and hypertrophy/strength" (55 studies):** **hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure** (non-linear). Sets at about 4+ RIR may give meaningfully less growth. **Strength gains were similar across a wide RIR range.** The authors suggest stopping about 3–5 reps short for strength. They call the analysis exploratory with modest model fit. [V] https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study ; https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295
- **Refalo 2024 (J Sports Sci), within-subject RCT in trained lifters:** 0 RIR vs 1–2 RIR gave similar quad hypertrophy over 8 weeks. Failure is not needed when stopping 1–2 RIR. [S]
- **Rule:** hypertrophy sets at **0–3 RIR**, mostly 1–2. Reserve 0 RIR (failure) for the last set of isolations or machines, where it is safe and low-fatigue. Avoid failure on heavy axial compounds (squat, deadlift, heavy rows) because of fatigue cost and risk. Strength work at **1–4 RIR** (RPE 6–9).

### 1.5 Rest intervals
- **Singer, Wolf, Generoso, ... Schoenfeld 2024 (Front Sports Act Living 6:1429789), "Give it a rest":** Bayesian meta-analysis of 9 studies / 19 measurements. There is a **small hypertrophic benefit to rest >60 s**, likely because of more volume load. **No appreciable difference beyond about 90 s.** Whether sets went to failure did not change this. [V] https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text
- Older evidence (Schoenfeld 2016, 1 vs 3 min in trained men): 3 min gave more hypertrophy and strength. [S]
- **Rule:** compounds 2–3 min (up to 5 min for heavy strength sets). Isolations 60–120 s. Use supersets of antagonist or non-competing muscles to save time without cutting rest for the same muscle (Iversen 2021).

### 1.6 Range of motion and long-muscle-length training
- **Wolf et al. 2023 (Int J Strength Cond), "Partial vs Full ROM RT" MA:** overall SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26), a trivial edge for full ROM. **Subgroup: partials at long muscle lengths may beat full ROM** (effect about 0.28, CI crosses 0). Shortened partials are worse than full ROM. [V] https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182
- **Pedrosa et al. 2022 (Eur J Sport Sci):** leg extension lengthened partials (100–65° knee flexion) produced more distal quad growth than full ROM or shortened partials (untrained women). [V]
- **Kassiano et al. 2023 (JSCR):** calf raises. Lengthened partials > full ROM > shortened partials for gastrocnemius growth. [V]
- **Maeo et al. 2022 (Eur J Sport Sci):** overhead triceps extension (long head stretched) produced **substantially greater triceps hypertrophy** than neutral-arm extension (about 1.4x the growth for the whole muscle, and more for the long head). [V for direction, S for magnitude]
- **Maeo et al. 2021 (Med Sci Sports Exerc):** seated leg curl (hip flexed, so the hamstrings are lengthened) gave more hamstring growth than lying leg curl. [S]
- **Wolf et al. 2025 (PeerJ), within-participant trained lifters:** lengthened partials ≈ full ROM. The advantage is small to trivial in trained people. [V] https://peerj.com/articles/18904
- **Varovic/Wolf 2024 preprint MA ("training at longer muscle lengths"):** small effects favour longer muscle lengths. [V]
- **Rule:** choose exercises that load the muscle hard **in the stretched position** (RDLs, deep squats, incline curls, overhead triceps, seated leg curls, deficit push-ups, cable flyes, standing calf raises with a deep stretch). Use full ROM by default. Lengthened partials are a valid add-on (e.g. after failure on full ROM) but not a replacement requirement. **Avoid shortened-range partials** as primary work.

### 1.7 Exercise selection and variation
- **Kassiano et al. 2022 (JSCR 36:1753), systematic review (8 studies):** *systematic* variation (e.g. squat + leg press + lunge, chosen for complementary regional or biomechanical emphasis) can improve **regional hypertrophy** and dynamic strength. **Excessive or random rotation** (e.g. changing exercises every session through an app) may blunt gains, partly by limiting skill and progressive overload. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/
- **Costa et al. 2022 (JSCR):** varied vs fixed exercises gave similar strength in most comparisons. [S]
- Regional hypertrophy is real. Different exercises grow different regions (e.g. rectus femoris needs knee-extension work; squats grow vasti and glutes but not much rectus femoris or hamstrings). Hamstrings need a knee-flexion exercise *and* a hip-hinge exercise.
- **Rule:** 1–3 exercises per muscle per week, chosen to cover (a) different joint actions and regions and (b) a stretched-position exercise. Keep core exercises for at least one mesocycle (4–8+ weeks) so overload can be tracked. Rotate accessories every 1–3 mesocycles for joint comfort, boredom or stagnation. Prefer stable, machine or cable options for close-to-failure hypertrophy work. Use free-weight compounds for strength and skill.

## 2. Strength

- **Specificity (SAID):** strength gains are largest in the *trained task*. **A 2025 meta-analysis on task specificity** found dynamic strength rose with about a 2x larger effect size than untrained isometric strength. High-load training wins for 1RM (Schoenfeld 2017; Lopez 2021; Currier 2023). [V] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12296774/
- **Intensity:** 1RM-focused work mostly at **70–90%+ 1RM (1–6 reps, RPE 6–9)**. Practise heavy singles or doubles (≥90%) in the 2–4 weeks before testing or competition. Swinton, Schoenfeld & Murphy 2024 dose-response MA: strength gains are maximised with high loads, plus enough sets. [S]
- **Proximity to failure:** largely irrelevant for strength within about 0–5 RIR (Robinson 2024). Leave reps in the tank for bar speed, technique and fatigue control.
- **Volume:** pronounced diminishing returns (Pelland 2025). A minimum effective dose of about 3–6 heavy sets/lift/week (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021) gives meaningful 1RM gains in trained powerlifters. Typical intermediate programmes use about 6–15 working sets per main lift per week, including variations.
- **Frequency:** a clear positive effect independent of volume (Pelland 2025). Earlier volume-equated MAs (Grgic 2018) found no difference, so the effect is modest. **Squat, bench and deadlift 2–3x/week** each (bench often 3–4x, deadlift 1–2x) is the common evidence-consistent practice. [V/S] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470825
- **Periodization (Moesgaard et al. 2022, Sports Med 52:1647):** volume-equated periodised training beat non-periodised for **1RM (ES 0.31)** but not for hypertrophy (ES 0.13, ns). **Undulating > linear for 1RM (ES 0.31)**, with no hypertrophy difference. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/
- **Peaking basics (powerlifting practice; Pritchard/Travis taper reviews) [S/P]:**
  - Final block of 3–6 weeks: specificity rises (competition lifts at 85–95%+, low reps), volume falls.
  - Taper of about 1–2 weeks: **cut volume about 30–70%, keep intensity high, keep frequency**. Last heavy single (≈90–95%) about 7–10 days out. Last session 2–4 days out is light.
  - Test or attempt selection: opener ≈ a weight you can triple, or about 90–92% of goal; second ≈ 95–98%; third = PR attempt.

## 3. Minimum effective dose (busy people / beginners)

- **Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher & Steele 2020 (Sports Med), SR/MA in resistance-trained men:** **a single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3x/week, for 8–12 weeks** produced meaningful 1RM gains in the squat and bench. This was the minimum dose, and more is better. [S; the dose is confirmed as cited in Iversen 2021]
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 (Front Sports Act Living), powerlifters:** **about 3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week**, across 1–3 sessions, at **>80% 1RM, RPE 7.5–9.5**, for 6–12 weeks. Autoregulated singles at RPE 9–9.5 are the most time-efficient option but less reliable. Adding 2–3 back-off sets at about 80% of the single helps. [V] https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/text
- **Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland 2021 (Sports Med 51:2079), "No Time to Lift?":** [V] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772
  - Prioritise **weekly volume ≥4 sets/muscle/week** (hypertrophy). Frequency matters less.
  - Use **bilateral, multi-joint** exercises (leg press/squat, chest press, row/pulldown, etc.).
  - Sets of **6–15 reps** taken close to failure.
  - Use **supersets (agonist–antagonist), drop sets and rest-pause** to roughly halve session time for the same volume.
  - Limit warm-up to **exercise-specific warm-up sets**. Skip general cardio warm-up and stretching.
- **Steele et al. 2022/2023 (long-term minimal dose, about 14,690 gym members):** 1x/week, single sets to failure on about 6 machines gave large strength gains in year 1, which plateaued around 1–2 years. [V]
- **Population guidelines:** WHO 2020 recommends muscle-strengthening activity on **≥2 days/week** involving all major muscle groups (plus aerobic activity). [V] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/
- **Coaching template (about 2 x 30–45 min/week):** full body A/B. 1 squat or leg-press pattern, 1 hinge, 1 horizontal push, 1 vertical or horizontal pull, plus optional arm or calf superset. **2–3 hard sets each, 6–15 reps, 1–3 RIR.** That is about 4–6 sets/muscle/week, which gives most of the beginner gains.

## 4. Progression models

### 4.1 Training-age definitions [P, practitioner-based; no consensus definition exists]
- **Beginner / novice:** <about 6–12 months of consistent structured training, *or* still able to add load **session to session** or week to week. Technique is still developing.
- **Intermediate:** about 1–3(+) years. Progress happens **week to week or over a mesocycle**. Needs structured volume and intensity variation.
- **Advanced:** 3–5+ years, close to genetic potential. Progress is **monthly or per block**. Benefits from individualised volume, specialisation and periodisation.
- (Model from Rippetoe & Kilgore, *Practical Programming*. Helms et al. *Muscle & Strength Pyramid* use similar language. Research papers usually define "trained" as ≥1 year of RT and/or relative-strength thresholds, e.g. bench ≥1.0x BW and squat ≥1.25–1.5x BW in men.)

### 4.2 Linear progression (novices)
- Add load every session (or every week) while reps stay fixed. Typical increments: **lower body +2.5–5 kg per session, upper body +1–2.5 kg** (use smaller jumps or microplates for women and smaller lifters, and on overhead press).
- When a lift misses its target reps twice, reset about 10% and rebuild, or move that lift to double progression or weekly progression.

### 4.3 Double progression (default for hypertrophy and accessories)
- Prescribe a **rep range**, e.g. 3 sets x 8–12 @ 1–2 RIR.
- Keep the load until **all sets hit the top of the range** (or the first set exceeds it) at the target RIR. Then add the smallest increment (about 2–5% or 1–2.5 kg) and drop back to the bottom of the range.
- Variants: "rep goal" (e.g. 36 total reps over 3 sets). Load-drop sets.

### 4.4 RPE / RIR autoregulation
- **Zourdos et al. 2016 (JSCR 30:267–275):** RIR-based RPE scale. **RPE 10 = 0 RIR (failure or max). 9 = 1 RIR. 8 = 2 RIR. 7 = 3 RIR. 6 = 4+ RIR (fast or warm-up). Half points (9.5 = maybe 1 more rep / could add a small load).** Inverse relationship with bar velocity. [V] https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/efef3b25-6701-4fb5-bb82-55fcd2a26027/full
- **Accuracy:** RIR estimates get **more accurate closer to failure** (≤3 RIR), with **lower-rep sets**, heavier loads and more experienced lifters. Lifters typically underestimate RIR by about 1 rep (they think they are closer to failure than they are). Accuracy falls for high-rep, light-load sets (Zourdos 2021; Halperin 2022 scoping review; Russo 2025 SR). [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747900/
- **Calibration tip:** novices should occasionally take a safe isolation or machine set to true failure to calibrate RIR. Coach rule: if the RIR prediction is off by >1–2 reps, prescribe *more conservative* targets until calibrated.
- **Helms et al. 2018 (Front Physiol):** RPE-based load prescription vs %1RM in trained men gave similar or slightly better strength gains with RPE. [S]
- **Implementation (RTS / Helms style):** top set @ RPE 8 for x reps. Then either back-off sets at −5–10% load, or "load drop" or "repeat to RPE 9" to self-regulate volume.

### 4.5 e1RM tracking
- **Epley:** e1RM = w × (1 + reps/30). **Brzycki:** e1RM = w × 36 / (37 − reps). Both are reliable for ≤10 reps. Accuracy falls with higher reps.
- **RPE-adjusted:** reps-to-failure = reps + RIR. Use e1RM = w × (1 + (reps + RIR)/30), or use the RTS RPE percentage chart. Approximate %1RM for (reps + RIR) = 1→100%, 2→~95%, 3→~92%, 4→~89%, 5→~86%, 6→~84%, 8→~79%, 10→~74%, 12→~70%.
- Track e1RM of the **top set** per lift per session. Use a rolling 3-session average to smooth day-to-day variability (±3–5% is normal).
- Plateau rule: no rise in e1RM for **≥3 weeks** (intermediate) or 2–3 sessions (novice) means a stall.

### 4.6 What to do when stalled (decision ladder)
1. **Check recovery inputs first:** sleep (≥7 h), calorie intake (a deficit makes stalls expected), protein, life stress, illness.
2. **Check execution:** consistency (are ≥90% of sessions completed?), technique, true RIR (are sets actually hard enough?).
3. **Fatigue-driven stall** (performance dropping, joints achy, motivation low, RPE rising at the same load): **deload** for 1 week, then resume at a slightly lower starting point.
4. **Stimulus-driven stall** (feels fresh, RPE fine, just not progressing): **add volume** (+2–4 sets/week to the muscle or lift), or add frequency (spread the same volume across one more session).
5. Change rep range or load scheme (e.g. 3x5 to 4x8 for hypertrophy and work capacity, or DUP: heavy/medium/light days).
6. **Swap the exercise variation** (close variant: pause squat, close-grip bench, different machine) after 2+ mesocycles of stagnation.
7. Novice moving to intermediate: switch from session-to-session LP to weekly progression (e.g. Texas Method, 5/3/1, GZCL or RIR blocks).

## 5. Deloads, fatigue management, overreaching

- **Definition (Bell et al. 2023 Delphi, Sports Med Open; 3 rounds, 34 → 21 coaches):** "Deloading is a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training." [V] https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417/
- **Practice survey (Rogerson, Nolan, Korakakis, Immonen, Wolf, Bell 2024, Sports Med Open; n=246 strength/physique athletes):** typical deload = **6.4 ± 1.7 days, every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks**. **Volume reduced** (fewer sets and reps per set). **Intensity of effort (RPE/RIR) and load reduced**. **Frequency and exercise selection unchanged**. Main reasons are fatigue and energy management. About 48% added massage, stretching or foam rolling. [V] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10948666/
- **RCT, Coleman et al. 2024 (PeerJ 12:e16777; n=39 trained):** a 9-week programme with a **1-week complete training cessation** at week 5 vs continuous training. **Hypertrophy: no difference. Lower-body strength: slightly worse with the cessation "deload".** Readiness-to-train scores were slightly better in the continuous group. Conclusion: **taking a week fully off is unnecessary for growth and may slightly hurt strength. Prefer active (reduced) deloads.** [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/
- **Rule for the skill:**
  - **Scheduled:** every 4–8 weeks (beginners rarely need them for the first 3–6 months; advanced lifters or high-volume blocks need them every 4–6 weeks).
  - **Reactive (preferred trigger):** deload when ≥2 of these persist for ≥1–2 weeks: e1RM or reps falling at the same RIR; RPE climbing more than 1 point at the same loads; persistent soreness or joint pain; poor sleep, low motivation, irritability; elevated resting HR or depressed HRV (individual baseline); illness.
  - **How:** 5–7 days. **Volume −30–50%** (e.g. halve the sets). **Load −10–20%, or the same load at 3–4+ RIR**. Keep frequency and exercise selection. No failure sets.
- **Overreaching / overtraining evidence:**
  - **Grandou et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50:815), SR of 22 studies:** the **only reliable marker of overtraining in RT is a sustained performance decrement**. Hormonal and biochemical markers are inconsistent. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/
  - **Bell et al. 2020 (J Sports Sci), scoping review:** short high-volume or high-intensity blocks induce **functional overreaching** (recovered within days to 2 weeks, with supercompensation possible). Chronic excess can cause **non-functional overreaching** (weeks to months). **True overtraining syndrome is rarely documented in RT.** [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32602418/
  - Practical: planned short overreaching (1–2 weeks of higher volume) followed by a deload is a legitimate intermediate or advanced tool. Monitor with performance (e1RM, reps at fixed load), session RPE and wellness questionnaires (sleep, soreness, mood, stress, motivation on a 1–5 scale).

## 6. Templates and splits by training age and availability

General principles: **choose the split by the days the client can reliably train**, then distribute 10–20 sets per muscle across ≥2 exposures per week. All splits produce similar hypertrophy when volume is equated (Ramos-Campo 2024 meta-analysis on split vs full body: no meaningful difference). [S]

### 6.1 Full body, 2–3x/week (beginners; anyone with ≤3 days)
- 3 days (Mon/Wed/Fri), A/B alternating:
  - A: Squat 3x5–8 · Bench 3x6–10 · Row 3x8–12 · RDL 2x8–10 · Lateral raise 2x12–20 · Curl 2x10–15
  - B: Deadlift or trap-bar 2–3x3–6 · Overhead press 3x6–10 · Lat pulldown/pull-up 3x8–12 · Split squat/leg press 2–3x8–12 · Triceps overhead 2x10–15 · Calf raise 2x10–15
- Per muscle about 6–12 sets/week. Novice LP on main lifts, double progression on accessories. Sessions take 45–75 min.
- 2 days: same structure, 3 sets per movement. About 6–8 sets/muscle/week is enough for beginners.

### 6.2 Upper/Lower, 4x/week (late beginner to intermediate; the best general default)
- Upper 1 (strength bias): Bench 3–4x4–6 · Row 3–4x6–8 · OHP 2–3x6–10 · Pulldown 2–3x8–12 · Arms 2x10–15 each
- Lower 1: Squat 3–4x4–6 · RDL 3x6–10 · Leg curl (seated) 2–3x10–15 · Calf 3x8–15 · Abs
- Upper 2 (hypertrophy bias): Incline DB press 3x8–12 · Chest-supported row 3x8–12 · Cable fly 2–3x12–15 · Lateral raise 3x12–20 · Overhead triceps / incline curl 2–3x10–15
- Lower 2: Deadlift or hip thrust 3x5–8 · Leg press / hack squat 3x8–12 · Leg extension 2–3x10–15 · Leg curl 2–3x10–15 · Calf
- About 12–18 sets/muscle/week, each muscle 2x/week.

### 6.3 Push/Pull/Legs (intermediate to advanced; 3 or 6 days)
- PPL x2 (6 days) gives about 14–24 sets/muscle/week, 2x frequency. Best for high-volume advanced lifters with time and recovery capacity.
- PPL x1 (3 days) is only 1x/week per muscle. It is fine for maintenance, but for growth prefer full body 3x or a 5-day hybrid.
- Hybrid 5-day: Upper / Lower / Push / Pull / Legs.

### 6.4 Selecting by availability
| Days/week | Novice | Intermediate | Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full body x2 | Full body x2 (higher sets) | Full body x2 (maintenance or priority muscles) |
| 3 | Full body x3 | Full body x3 or Upper/Lower/Full | Full body x3 with specialisation |
| 4 | Upper/Lower (or FB x4) | Upper/Lower | Upper/Lower or body-part-focused U/L |
| 5 | U/L + extra FB or arms | U/L/PPL hybrid | PPL + U/L |
| 6 | Not needed | PPL x2 | PPL x2 / specialisation |

### 6.5 Strength templates (evidence-consistent)
- Novice: Starting-Strength / GZCLP-style LP, 3x/week full body.
- Intermediate: Texas Method, 5/3/1 (+ BBB / FSL), GZCL, nSuns, RTS-style RPE blocks with DUP (heavy/volume/light).
- Advanced: block periodisation (accumulation, then intensification, then realisation/peak), with RPE/e1RM autoregulation.

## 7. Concurrent training, cardio, warm-up and mobility

### 7.1 Interference effect: current view
- **Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:601–612):** updated MA. Concurrent aerobic + strength training **does not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength**, independent of aerobic type, frequency, training status and age. **Explosive strength (jump or RFD) is mildly attenuated**, especially when both are done in the same session (SMD about −0.28; secondary figure). [V for main conclusion] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34878640/
- **Lundberg, Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Med):** fiber-level analysis. Concurrent training blunted **type I fiber hypertrophy when the aerobic mode was running, not cycling**. [V]
- **Wilson et al. 2012 (JSCR):** 21 studies. Running (not cycling) reduced hypertrophy and strength gains. Interference scales with **endurance frequency (r ≈ −0.26 to −0.35) and duration (r ≈ −0.29 to −0.75)**. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/
- **Petré et al. 2021 (Sports Med):** interference with **max strength appeared only when both were in the same session**, not when they were separated (>2–3 h), and was more evident in trained individuals. [V/S]
- **Rule:** cardio is compatible with lifting. To minimise interference: (1) separate cardio and lifting by ≥3–6 h or put them on separate days. If they share a session, **lift first** when strength or hypertrophy is the priority. (2) Prefer cycling, rowing, incline walking or swimming over high-volume running. (3) Avoid hard intervals or long runs right before leg days. (4) Keep high-intensity cardio to about 2–3 sessions/week if hypertrophy is the priority. (5) Eat enough (energy deficit is a bigger interference factor than cardio itself).

### 7.2 Cardio guidelines
- **WHO 2020:** adults **150–300 min/week moderate** or **75–150 min/week vigorous** aerobic activity (or an equivalent mix), plus muscle-strengthening on **≥2 days/week**. Some activity is better than none. [V] https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/
- **Zone 2 / low-intensity steady state:** roughly at or below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold. In practice that is **about 60–70% HRmax** (or about 65–75% for trained people), nasal-breathing, conversational pace. It builds aerobic base, mitochondrial density and fat oxidation, recovers well and causes little interference. Current evidence (2023–2025 reviews and debate, e.g. Storoschuk et al. 2024) shows that **"Zone 2" is not uniquely superior** to other intensities for mitochondrial or VO2max adaptations in untrained or recreational people. Its value is that it is **low-fatigue, sustainable volume**. [S]
- **Intensity distribution:** endurance athletes use about 80% low / 20% high ("polarised" or pyramidal; Seiler). For lifters, a sensible mix is **2–3 x 30–60 min low intensity + 1 x HIIT/week**. [S]
- **HIIT / VO2max:** the **Norwegian 4x4** (Helgerud 2007) is 4 x 4 min at **85–95% HRmax** with 3 min active recovery, 1–2x/week. HIIT generally improves VO2max more than moderate continuous training per minute (Milanović 2015 MA, about +5.5 ml/kg/min). VO2max is one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality. [S]
- **Rule for lifters:** minimum health dose ≈ 150 min/week moderate (e.g. 3 x 30–40 min Z2 + 1 x 4x4). For fat-loss phases, increase daily steps (7–10k+) before adding much high-intensity cardio (NEAT is low-fatigue).

### 7.3 Warm-up and mobility
- **RAMP framework (Jeffreys 2007):** **R**aise (body temperature, HR: 3–5 min easy cardio), **A**ctivate and **M**obilise (dynamic movements for the session's joints), **P**otentiate (progressively heavier specific sets). [S/P]
- **For lifting specifically:** general warm-up is optional. **Specific warm-up sets** matter most (Iversen 2021; Ribeiro 2020). Example for a top set of 100 kg x 5: bar x 10, 40 x 5, 60 x 3, 75 x 2, 85–90 x 1, then working sets. Use fewer warm-up sets for isolation exercises (1–2).
- **Static stretching:** **Warneke & Lohmann 2024 (J Sport Health Sci) MA, 83 studies:** pre-exercise static stretching causes a **small** max-strength loss, mainly with holds **≥60 s per muscle**. **No impairment (or even positive effects) on jump and sprint performance**. Recommendations to *ban* static stretching from warm-ups are not supported. **Short holds (<60 s per muscle) followed by dynamic activity are fine.** [V] https://doaj.org/article/c9ed6653d18647408f444d101de5e38f
- **Warneke, Behm et al. 2025 Delphi consensus (J Sport Health Sci 14:101067):** international expert consensus on stretching (acute and chronic effects). Stretching is effective for ROM. Its effects on injury prevention are unclear. [V partial]
- **Chronic stretching and flexibility:** both stretching and **full-ROM resistance training improve ROM similarly** (Afonso 2021 MA). Static stretching alone causes little to no hypertrophy at typical doses; long-duration, high-intensity stretching (≥15 min/day) shows small effects in some studies. Chronic stretching has a trivial effect on jump (ES 0.16) and none on sprint (Warneke 2024 Frontiers). [V/S] https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1372689/full
- **Rule:** 5 min general warm-up if cold or early morning; dynamic mobility for the joints being trained (2–5 min); then ramped specific warm-up sets. Do static stretching for flexibility goals separately (post-workout or at other times, **2–4 x 30 s per muscle, ≥2–3x/week**, or via loaded stretches like deep RDLs and flyes).

## 8. Recovery and nutrition

### 8.1 Sleep
- **Saner et al. 2020 (J Physiol):** 5 nights of **4 h time-in-bed** lowered myofibrillar protein synthesis. High-intensity interval exercise maintained it at control levels. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32052430/ (abstract via https://zendy.io/title/10.1113/jp278828)
- **Lamon et al. 2021 (Physiol Rep):** one night of total sleep deprivation reduced MPS by about 18%, raised cortisol and lowered testosterone. [S]
- **Knowles et al. 2018 / Craven 2022 reviews:** sleep restriction impairs *multi-joint* strength and volume more than single-joint (Gentil review). [S] https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Inadequate%20sleep%20and%20muscle%20strength%20-%20Implications%20for%20resistance%20training.pdf
- Guideline (AASM/SRS): adults **≥7 h/night**. Athletes often benefit from 8–9 h. Naps of 20–30 min can partially offset short nights.

### 8.2 Protein
- **Morton et al. 2018 (BJSM 52:376), 49 RCTs, n=1,863:** protein supplementation increases RT gains in FFM (about +0.3 kg) and 1RM. **Breakpoint at 1.62 g/kg/day total protein (95% CI 1.03–2.20)**. Beyond this there is no further FFM benefit on average. **Recommending up to 2.2 g/kg covers the upper CI** (the origin of "1.6–2.2 g/kg"). [V] https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376
- **ISSN Position Stand 2017 (Jäger et al., JISSN 14:20):** **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising individuals building or maintaining muscle. **2.3–3.1 g/kg/day of lean mass** may be needed to maximise lean mass retention in resistance-trained people in hypocaloric phases (Helms 2014). Per meal **0.25 g/kg (≈20–40 g)** high-quality protein containing **700–3,000 mg leucine**. Distribute evenly **every 3–4 h**. Pre-sleep casein **30–40 g** is acceptable. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/
- **Nunes et al. 2022 (J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle) MA:** protein ≥1.6 g/kg/day with RT increases lean mass, with similar results in younger and older adults (older adults benefit from more per meal, about 0.4 g/kg). [S]
- **Trommelen et al. 2023 (Cell Rep Med):** 100 g protein produced a larger and more prolonged anabolic response than 25 g. The "~25–30 g per meal max" cap is a myth for total-body anabolism. Total daily intake matters most. [S]
- **Rule:** default target **1.6–2.2 g/kg BW/day** (use target or goal body weight, or about 2.0–2.4 g/kg lean mass, for people with obesity). **Cutting:** aim for the upper end (2.0–2.4 g/kg BW). Spread across **3–5 meals of ≥0.3–0.4 g/kg**.

### 8.3 Energy balance and recomposition
- **Barakat et al. 2020 (Strength Cond J 42(5):7–21), "Body Recomposition: Can Trained Individuals Build Muscle and Lose Fat at the Same Time?":** yes. Recomp is well documented in **novices, detrained or returning lifters, people with overweight or obesity, and even trained lifters**, given (1) progressive RT, (2) **high protein (≈1.6–2.4 g/kg)** and (3) a **modest energy status** (small deficit to maintenance). Sleep is also an important factor. [S] DOI 10.1519/SSC.0000000000000584
- **Rate guidelines [S/P]:**
  - **Fat loss:** deficit of about 300–500 kcal/day, aiming for **0.5–1.0% BW loss per week**. Slower (≈0.5%) preserves more lean mass in lean or trained people (Helms 2014; Garthe 2011). Keep training volume and intensity high and cut cardio before lifting volume.
  - **Lean gain ("lean bulk"):** surplus about **+5–15% (≈200–400 kcal/day)**. Target weight gain **≈0.25–0.5% BW/week** (novices toward the upper end, advanced lifters about 0.1–0.25%; Iraki et al. 2019 bodybuilder off-season review). A larger surplus adds mostly fat (Helms 2023 surplus RCT: a large surplus gave no extra muscle in trained lifters).
  - **Recomp:** maintenance to −10% intake, high protein, progressive training. Track with a weekly average of body weight plus waist and progress photos plus performance, not daily scale weight.

### 8.4 Creatine
- **ISSN Position Stand 2017 (Kreider et al., JISSN 14:18):** creatine monohydrate is the **most effective ergogenic supplement** for high-intensity exercise capacity and lean mass during training. **Safe** in healthy people (no evidence of kidney harm at recommended doses). Dosing is **3–5 g/day** maintenance. Optional loading of **0.3 g/kg/day (≈20 g in 4 doses) for 5–7 days** saturates stores faster. Without loading, saturation takes about 3–4 weeks. Timing is not important. Take it daily, possibly with a meal. [S/V]
- **Burke et al. 2023 (Nutrients), Bayesian MA, 10 studies / 44 outcomes (MRI, CT, US):** creatine + RT gave a **small increase in direct hypertrophy** (ES 0.11, 95% CrI −0.02 to 0.25). Upper and lower body muscle thickness increased about 0.10–0.16 cm. The effect was slightly larger in younger adults. [V] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/
- **Lean-mass MAs (e.g. Delpino 2022; Chilibeck 2017 in older adults):** about +0.7–1.4 kg extra lean mass vs placebo (partly water). Strength is higher by about +4–5 kg on bench and +10 kg on squat in pooled data (Lanhers 2015/2017). [S]
- Non-responders exist (about 20–30%, often those with high baseline intake, e.g. meat-heavy diets). Early weight gain of about 1–2 kg is intracellular water, not fat.

## 10. Caveats for the skill to communicate
- Most RT studies are short (6–12 weeks), run on young untrained or recreationally trained men, and use small samples. Individual responses vary widely.
- Volume numbers depend on *how sets are counted* (fractional vs direct). Always state the counting method.
- RIR self-reports are imprecise (±1–2 reps), especially at high reps and for novices.
- The long-length / lengthened-partials evidence is promising but the effect is small in trained lifters. Do not oversell it.
- The deload evidence base is thin (one RCT of a *full training break*, plus a survey and a Delphi). Recommendations are largely expert consensus.
- For medical conditions, pregnancy, injuries or adolescents, refer the client to qualified professionals.
